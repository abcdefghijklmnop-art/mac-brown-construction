### What is a transverse wave curve cast?

A **transverse wave curve cast** introduces a wave into the unrolling loop by moving the rod tip perpendicular to the target — either during the casting stroke or after the rod stops. Moving the rod tip out slow and recovering fast produces a large curve to the right. Moving out fast and recovering slowly curves the opposite direction. The direction and rate of the snap movement control which curve you produce.

---

### What is a circle cast and how does it create a curve?

A **circle cast** maintains constant tension throughout the stroke by rotating the rod in a circular path rather than a straight back-and-forth motion. To produce a curve, implement **rod fade** — a deliberate lowering of the rod toward the target — near the delivery point. A counterclockwise rotation with a brief stall before delivery produces a large curve to the right. Accenting the forward delivery from the same motion produces a large curve to the left.

---

### What is rod fade and why does it matter for curve casts?

**Rod fade** is a deliberate reduction of rod tip speed and rotation during the final delivery stroke — used to underpow the loop so it fails to fully straighten and falls in a curve. It is the core technique behind **negative curve casts** and is what separates a controlled presentational curve from an accidental tailing loop. Without rod fade, negative curves land randomly. With it, the caster controls the size and direction of the curve consistently.

---

### What is a skewed line curve cast?

A **skewed line curve cast** deviates intentionally from the 180-degree rule by angling the backcast away from the straight-line path of the forward delivery. This skew alters the forces acting on the forward cast and produces a curved layout. Al Buhr's **Cut Cast** — detailed in his book *Two-Handed Fly Casting* — is a practical fishing application of the skewed backcast that delivers the fly around obstacles with precise directional control.
