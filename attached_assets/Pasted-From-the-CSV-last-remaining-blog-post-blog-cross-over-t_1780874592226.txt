Q1: What are crossover techniques

A1: Crossover techniques apply the 
presentation methods of one fly 
fishing discipline to another —

nymph, and streamer fishing. A dry 
fly sunk in the film and swung 
through a seam is a wet fly 
presentation. A pheasant tail nymph 
swung into rising fish is a wet fly 
technique. A streamer dead-drifted 
in cold water is a nymphing 
technique. The most effective 
trout anglers think in presentations 
and intents — not in categories.

Q2: Can you swing nymphs to 
rising trout?
A2: Yes — and it is often more 
effective than a dry fly during 
certain hatch stages. When trout 
are rising to emergers in the film 
rather than fully formed adults 
on the surface, swinging a soft 
hackle or small pheasant tail 
nymph through the rise zone at 
the correct depth and speed 
consistently produces strikes 
that dry flies miss. Mac Brown 
used small pheasant tail nymphs 
swung into rising fish with 
excellent results — a classic 
crossover technique.

Q3: Is dry fly fishing really 
the highest form of fly fishing?
A3: That is dry fly snobbery — 
a form of elitism that does not 
serve the angler or the sport. 
Dry fly fishing is the most 
visually exciting method and 
requires refined line control 
developed over many seasons. 
But it is not superior to nymph, 
wet fly, or streamer fishing — 
it is simply different. The 
complete fly fisher uses all 
methods at the right time for 
the right conditions. Technique 
elitism is the enemy of skill 
development.

Q4: Is indicator fishing really 
fly fishing?
A4: Yes — the debate over whether 
indicators, Tenkara, or various 
nymphing styles constitute real 
fly fishing is a distraction from 
what matters: catching fish, 
understanding water, and 
developing presentation skill. 
The divisions between what counts 
as fly fishing are largely 
invented by opinion rather than 
based on any meaningful standard. 
Mac Brown teaches all methods 
and encourages students to 
borrow freely across disciplines 
to solve problems on the water.
