FAQ — add before footer as expandable accordion:

Q1: What is a fly casting model?
A1: A fly casting model is a structured 
framework of terms and concepts used to 
describe every element of the fly cast in 
a way that applies universally across all 
casting styles — single-hand, switch, and 
Spey. It distinguishes between a casting 
stroke (which generates a loop) and an 
overall stroke (which includes all rod 
movements). A good model gives instructors 
and students a shared language for 
diagnosing faults, discussing mechanics, 
and advancing skill without relying on 
rules that break down in real fishing 
conditions.

Q2: What is the difference between a 
casting stroke and an overall stroke?
A2: A casting stroke covers only those 
rod motions whose purpose is to apply 
force to the line and generate a loop — 
it has one result: a loop of line. An 
overall stroke or just stroke includes 
all rod movements during the casting 
sequence, including positioning moves 
like lifts, sweeps, and mends that do 
not directly generate a loop. Keeping 
these two concepts separate is essential 
for accurate fault diagnosis and 
instruction.

Q3: Why do fly casting rules break down?
A3: Most fly casting rules are 
observations about one style or 
situation that get applied universally 
where they do not belong. Rules about 
rod arc, elbow position, wrist 
involvement, and acceleration work 
in some contexts and fail in others — 
because style is personal and situation-
dependent while mechanics are universal. 
A model based on principles rather than 
rules gives the caster and instructor 
tools that work across all casting 
planes, styles, and fishing scenarios.

Q4: How does the fly casting model 
apply to Spey casting?
A4: The model works particularly well 
with Spey casts because it accommodates 
change of direction casts, constant 
tension casts, and the full range of 
anchor placement and D-loop positioning 
without forcing them into a single-hand 
framework. The distinction between 
casting stroke and positioning moves 
is especially valuable for teaching 
Spey — where most of the sequence is 
positioning before the actual delivery.
