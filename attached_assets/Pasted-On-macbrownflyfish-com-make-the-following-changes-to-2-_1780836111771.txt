PAGE 1: /blog/barbless-hooks-increase-your-catch/

AEO — add immediately below H1:
"Barbless hooks increase catch rates in fly 
fishing because they penetrate faster with 
less effort than barbed hooks — a spear versus 
a wedge. Mac Brown has guided exclusively with 
barbless hooks in Bryson City, NC since 1987, 
finding they lead to higher fish numbers, 
reduced handling time, and significantly lower 
fish mortality on delayed harvest water. The 
cardboard test proves it: compare the effort 
required to push a barbed hook versus a 
barbless hook through cardboard — the 
difference is immediate and obvious."

FAQ — add before footer as expandable accordion:
Q1: Do barbless hooks really increase your 
catch rate?
A1: Yes — barbless hooks penetrate faster 
with less effort than barbed hooks because 
the point is not fighting against a barb to 
drive home. Think of it as the difference 
between a spear and a wedge. The masses of 
fly fishers believe a barbed hook keeps fish 
on better — but a better hookset technique 
is what actually keeps fish on, not the barb.

Q2: Do fish come off more easily on barbless 
hooks?
A2: Fish come off barbless hooks when the 
angler creates slack — not because of the 
hook design. Maintain a tight line and proper 
rod angle and the fish stays on. As a guide, 
Mac Brown finds it much easier to simply give 
a bit of slack and have the barbless hook fall 
out cleanly at the net — leading to faster 
releases and more fish landed throughout the 
day with far less handling.

Q3: What are the best barbless hooks for 
fly tying?
A3: Hanak, Fulling Mill, Dohiku, and Maturo 
produce excellent quality barbless wire that 
was not available to fly tiers years ago. 
These hooks ride inverted — hook up — and 
consistently take fish in the upper part of 
the mouth for a solid hook set. The quality 
of wire in modern barbless hooks is 
significantly better than older barbed hooks 
from traditional manufacturers.

Q4: Are barbless hooks required in Great 
Smoky Mountains National Park?
A4: Yes — artificial lures with single 
barbless hooks are required in Great Smoky 
Mountains National Park. The park's wild 
trout water is catch and release only with 
artificial flies and lures. Barbless hooks 
are not just an ethical choice on this water 
— they are the law.

Q5: How do I make a barbed hook barbless?
A5: Use hemostats or pliers to flatten the 
barb down toward the hook point — you do not 
need to remove it entirely, just get it flat 
enough that it no longer grips. Japanese 
chemically sharpened hooks with micro barbs 
are the easiest to crimp. Work slowly from 
the rear of the barb forward. Test with your 
thumbnail — if it catches, keep crimping.

PAGE 2: /blog/learn-to-set-the-hook-fly-fishing-trout/

AEO — add immediately below H1:
"The vertical wrist-jerk hook set is the most 
common mistake in fly fishing trout — and the 
technique most likely to result in missed fish 
and broken tippets. Mac Brown has guided on 
the Tuckasegee and Nantahala rivers since 1987 
and teaches a horizontal strip-set as the 
highest percentage hook set for trout in all 
conditions. Both the rod hand and line hand 
have specific duties — and most anglers are 
only using one of them."

FAQ — add before footer as expandable accordion:
Q1: What is the best way to set the hook 
when fly fishing for trout?
A1: The highest percentage hook set for trout 
is a controlled horizontal strip-set — not 
the vertical wrist jerk most anglers use. 
Keep the rod tip low and strip the line firmly 
with the line hand while the rod hand 
maintains controlled pressure. The vertical 
jerk is difficult to control, hard on fine 
tippets, and produces the lowest hookup 
percentage of any technique.

Q2: Why do I keep missing strikes when 
fly fishing?
A2: Missing strikes is almost always a timing 
or technique issue — not hook size or pattern. 
The most common causes are setting too early 
before the fish has turned, setting vertically 
instead of horizontally, and failing to use 
the line hand in the set. On dry flies wait 
until you see the fish turn back down before 
setting. On nymphs set at any hesitation in 
the indicator or drift.

Q3: How do I set the hook on a dry fly 
without breaking the tippet?
A3: Use a controlled, short horizontal set 
rather than a hard vertical sweep. Fine 
tippets break on vertical sets because the 
wrist jerk is difficult to modulate — you 
either don't set hard enough or you snap the 
tippet. A horizontal strip-set gives you far 
more control over the amount of force applied 
and protects tippet down to 7X consistently.

Q4: How do I avoid dropping the rod tip 
when netting a fish?
A4: The most common way to drop a fish at 
the net is letting the rod straighten when 
reaching for the net. The shoulder naturally 
dips when the arm extends for the net — which 
drops the rod tip and creates slack. Keep the 
rod high and the line tight until the fish 
is fully in the net. Practice this motion 
before you need it.

Q5: Is the hook set different for nymphs, 
dry flies, and streamers?
A5: Yes — each requires a different approach. 
Dry flies need a short controlled set after 
the fish turns down. Nymphs require an 
immediate set at any pause or movement in 
the indicator — the takes are subtle and the 
window is very short in cold water. Streamers 
use a strip-set — strip through the grab, 
feel the hard connection, then lift the rod. 
Lifting on a streamer take before the fish 
is fully on is the most common streamer 
mistake.
