FAQ — add before footer as expandable accordion:
Q1: What is rod acceleration in fly casting?
A1: Rod acceleration is the rate of change 
in velocity applied to the rod during the 
casting stroke. It exists in three forms — 
negative (decelerating), constant (steady), 
and positive (accelerating). Most instruction 
addresses only constant acceleration, which 
is inadequate for controlling line and fly 
layout in real fishing conditions. Mastering 
all three gives the caster full control over 
every presentation cast on the water.

Q2: What is the difference between negative, 
constant, and positive rod acceleration?
A2: Constant acceleration maintains steady 
rod speed throughout the stroke — useful for 
standard distance casts. Positive acceleration 
increases speed progressively through the 
stroke, loading the rod deeper for tight 
loops and distance. Negative acceleration 
decreases speed — used deliberately for 
pile casts, slack leader presentations, 
and certain curve casts where the fly needs 
to land with controlled slack.

Q3: Why do most fly casters use too much 
acceleration?
A3: Most casters default to maximum effort 
because early instruction emphasizes getting 
the fly line out there rather than controlling 
where and how it lands. Overpowering the 
stroke produces tailing loops, poor layout, 
and loss of presentation control. The remedy 
is practicing at the slowest possible speeds 
to develop sensitivity to rod load — then 
building back up with intention.

Q4: What is jerk in fly casting?
A4: Jerk is a change in the rate of 
acceleration over time — the abrupt increase 
in speed that elite casters use near the stop 
to achieve maximum line speed and distance. 
The key is remaining smooth throughout the 
stroke while building to the jerk — a 
crescendo of speed that synchronizes with 
the haul, the stop, and the rod unbend 
simultaneously. The whole is greater than 
the sum of its parts.

Q5: Where can I learn advanced rod 
acceleration techniques?
A5: Mac Brown Fly Fish in Bryson City, NC 
offers private fly casting lessons and the 
Masterclass Casting Workshop — covering

loop control, and advanced presentation casts. 
Led by Mac Brown, the only FFI Master Casting

with Gary Borger of the All-Day Advanced 
Casting Class at The Fly Fishing Show.

FAQ — add before footer as expandable accordion:
Q1: What is the double haul in fly casting?
A1: The double haul is a line acceleration 
technique where the line hand pulls the fly 
line on both the back cast and the forward 
cast — distributing the workload between 
both hands and dramatically increasing line 
speed. It was introduced to the world by 
Marvin Hedge in 1934 when he broke the fly 
casting distance record by 22 feet. It is 
not just a distance technique — a properly 
timed double haul improves accuracy, loop 
control, and efficiency on every cast.

Q2: How do I learn the timing of the 
double haul?
A2: The fastest method is pantomime — 
practicing the haul motion with no rod or 
line in hand. Mel Krieger originated this 
approach in the mid-1970s teaching the 
Fenwick Fly Casting Schools, achieving 
results in under 10 minutes. Practice the 
down-up motion of the line hand repeatedly 
until the timing becomes automatic before 
adding the rod. Mac Brown's animated GIF 
on the double haul page at macbrownflyfish.com 
illustrates the correct sequence.

Q3: When should the haul begin during 
the casting stroke?
A3: The haul should begin around the halfway 
point through the casting stroke. Starting 
too early reduces the rod load benefit. 
Starting too late creates a jerky, 
disconnected cast. Think of the haul, the 
rod stop, and the rod unbend occurring 
simultaneously at the completion of the 
stroke — the synchronicity of all three 
is what produces maximum line speed with 
minimum effort.

Q4: Does the double haul help with 
short casts or just distance?
A4: Both — and most anglers underestimate 
how much the double haul improves short 
game casting. A properly timed haul 
produces tighter loops, better accuracy, 
and reduced false casting at any distance. 
It also protects fine tippets by allowing 
the same line speed with significantly 
less rod hand force. Every fly caster 
benefits from learning the double haul 
regardless of the distances they fish.

Q5: Where can I learn the double haul 
from a certified instructor?
A5: Mac Brown Fly Fish in Bryson City, NC

the only FFI Master Casting Instructor in 
North Carolina, who has taught the double 
haul for 38+ years. Online lessons via 
FaceTime, Zoom, or Skype are also available
