FAQ — add before footer as expandable accordion:

Q1: Do tight loops always mean more 
accurate fly casting?
A1: No — tight loops are efficient for 
distance and wind penetration but 
accuracy depends on trajectory, 
tracking, and line speed working 
together. A tight loop thrown with 
poor tracking lands in the wrong 
place with great efficiency. Accuracy 
requires the rod tip to travel in a 
consistent path that delivers the fly 
to the target — loop width is secondary 
to the direction and trajectory of that 
path.

Q2: What causes poor tracking in 
fly casting?
A2: The most common tracking fault is 
rolling the palm outward at the 
completion of the back cast stop — 
introducing a transverse wave into 
the fly leg of the loop that kicks 
horizontally and destroys forward cast 
accuracy. A right-handed caster with 
this fault will see the back cast loop 
kick hard right. Check your tracking 
by watching whether the reel points 
straight ahead toward the target at 
the stop of the back cast. If it 
points away perpendicular to the 
target, the back cast has a wave.

Q3: What is a transverse wave in 
fly casting?
A3: A transverse wave is an oscillation 
perpendicular to the direction of line 
travel — introduced into the fly leg 
of the loop by abrupt or errant rod 
movements during or just after the 
casting stroke. Transverse waves cause 
tailing loops, tracking errors, and 
inaccurate presentations. They can 
also be used deliberately — as in 
S-cast mends, pile casts, curve casts, 
and aerial mends — making them one of 
the most powerful tools in advanced 
fly casting once understood and 
controlled.

Q4: How do I improve fly casting 
accuracy?
A4: Start with the back cast — most 
accuracy problems originate there, 
not on the forward cast. Ensure the 
reel points straight ahead at the 
stop. Keep the casting plane 
consistent front to back. Practice 
at short distances first — 20-30 
feet — until the loop travels in a 
perfect straight plane in both 
directions. Then extend distance. 
Accuracy at distance is impossible 
without clean mechanics at short 
range.
