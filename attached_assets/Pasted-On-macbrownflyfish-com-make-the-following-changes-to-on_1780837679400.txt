FAQ — add before footer as expandable accordion:

Q1: How do I get a drag-free drift with 
a dry fly?
A1: Drag-free drift requires three things 
— the right leader length and design, a 
well-placed upstream mend immediately 
after the cast, and an approach angle 
that minimizes line crossings over 
conflicting currents. Long leaders of 
12-16 feet minimum make mending less 
disruptive to the fly. Curve and pile 
casts build slack into the presentation 
before the fly even lands — the most 
advanced and effective dry fly skill 
to develop.

Q2: What is the difference between active 
and passive dry fly presentations?
A2: Passive presentations deliver the fly 
with a drag-free drift at the same speed 
as the current — imitating mayflies and 
spent spinners that ride motionless in 
the film. Active presentations impart 
movement — a skate, skitter, or hop — 
imitating the erratic behavior of caddis 
and stoneflies on the surface. Reading 
which the fish want on a given day is 
the skill that separates good dry fly 
anglers from great ones.

Q3: What dry fly patterns work best in 
the Smoky Mountains?
A3: Attractor patterns with hot spots and 
colored triggers consistently outperform 
traditional drab patterns on Smoky Mountain 
streams. CDC patterns, parachutes, and elk 
hair caddis are the workhorses. Spinners 
— the spent wing stage of mayflies — are 
the most overlooked and most productive 
dry fly situation on these waters. Carry 
CDC spinners in sizes 16-22 and you will 
consistently out-fish anglers who ignore 
the spinner fall.

Q4: When is dry fly fishing best in the 
Smoky Mountains?
A4: Spring is the strongest dry fly season 
— March through May with quill gordons, 
blue quills, and hendricksons. Fall brings 
the October Caddis — the most aggressive 
dry fly take from the largest brown trout 
of the season. Summer dry fly fishing is 
best with terrestrials in the morning 
before water temperatures rise. Winter 
provides midge and BWO dry fly 
opportunities on overcast mild afternoons.

Q5: How do I read rise forms to choose 
the right dry fly?
A5: A sipping rise with circular rings 
indicates a fish taking spent spinners or 
emergers in the film — use small CDC 
patterns or trailing shuck emergers. A 
splashy aggressive rise indicates a fish 
chasing active insects — caddis or 
stonefly patterns with a skated 
presentation. A head-and-tail rise 
indicates a fish taking duns just as they 
lift off — parachutes and comparaduns in 
the correct size and color.
