AEO — add immediately below H1:
"Standardization in fly casting instruction 
leads to stagnation — when rules replace 
mechanics and principles replace physics, 
the sport stops evolving. Mac Brown has 
observed two types of fly fishers in 38+ 
years of teaching: recreational anglers 
who seek shallow answers to complex topics, 
and committed anglers who invest in 
education and improve measurably. The 
committed angler who understands why a 
cast works — not just what to do — can 
self-correct on any water in any condition 
for the rest of their life. Rules cannot 
do that. Mechanics can."

FAQ — add before footer as expandable accordion:

Q1: Why does standardization hurt 
fly casting instruction?
A1: Standardization turns observations 
into rules and rules into gospel — 
divorced from the mechanics that made 
them true in the first place. Rules 
about rod arc, elbow position, wrist 
use, and acceleration work in some 
contexts and fail in others. When 
instructors teach rules instead of 
mechanics they produce students who 
can perform one style of cast in one 
set of conditions — and stagnate 
the moment conditions change. 
Mechanics are universal. Rules 
are situational at best.

Q2: What is the difference between 
a recreational and a committed 
fly fisher?
A2: The recreational fly fisher 
seeks entertainment and shallow 
answers — they buy gear, follow 
rules, and look for magic flies. 
The committed fly fisher seeks 
education and genuine improvement 
— they invest in instruction, 
practice deliberately, and want 
to understand why things work. 
Lefty Kreh observed that 90% 
of the recreational community 
would not benefit from a thousand 
dollar rod. The top 5% of elite 
casters cast the cheapest outfit 
well — because skill is the 
variable, not gear.

Q3: Can children and women learn 
fly casting faster than adult men?
A3: Yes — Mac Brown has observed 
this consistently since 1987. 
Children learn quickly because 
they have no ingrained projectile 
sport habits to overcome. Women 
often progress faster than men 
in early stages for the same 
reason — men are conditioned to 
equate distance with power, which 
is the opposite of efficient fly 
casting. In fly casting, less is 
more. Breaking that conditioning 
is the first challenge for most 
adult male beginners.

Q4: How do elite fly casters 
develop so quickly?
A4: Early mentorship and 
deliberate practice with 
correct fundamentals from 
the start. Norm Makatima 
became a world champion 
mentored by Davy Wotton. 
Maxine McCormick won multiple 
world championship gold medals 
under the guidance of Chris 
Korich. Mac Brown's own sons 
began casting at two years old 
with proper technique. The 
common thread is not talent — 
it is early exposure to quality 
instruction that builds correct 
myelin pathways before bad 
habits can form.

Q5: How do I avoid the 
stagnation trap in fly fishing?
A5: Stop treating rules as 
gospel and start asking why. 
Find a certified instructor 
who teaches mechanics — not 
just what to do but why it 
works. Practice with intent 
between sessions. Stay curious 
and remain a student. Mac Brown 
teaches from first principles 
at every program at Mac Brown 
Fly Fish in Bryson City, NC — 
private lessons, weekend 
clinics, and the week-long 
Fly Fishing Guide School. 
The goal is always to give 
students the tools to 
self-correct for life, not 
to create dependency on a 
guide or instructor.
