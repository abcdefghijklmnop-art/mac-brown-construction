"The key to making an efficient roll cast 
in fly fishing is pulling the rod butt 
straight downward — not pushing through 
with forced turnover at the end of the 
stroke. The pulling motion begins from 
the shoulder, drops through the elbow, 
and completes with a positive stop and 
pullback from the pinky and ring finger — 
similar to driving a large spike with a 
hammer or throwing an ax. The result is 
a flat fly leg with a narrow loop that 
carries great distances with minimal 
effort. Less is more. Mac Brown has been 
teaching the static roll cast in Bryson 
City, NC since 1987."

FAQ — add before footer as expandable accordion:

Q1: What is the difference between 
pulling and pushing in a roll cast?
A1: Pushing through the roll cast — 
forced turnover — rotates everything 
at the end of the stroke and produces 
wasted residual energy in waves 
traveling down the rod leg, wide 
loops, and poor distance. Pulling 
the rod butt straight downward begins 
rotation from the start of the stroke 
in a controlled downward fashion — 
producing a flat fly leg, a narrow 
loop, and far greater distance with 
minimal effort. Al Buhr calls this 
the forced turnover versus pulling 
distinction — one of the most 
important mechanical differences 
in fly casting.

Q2: What is a positive stop in 
fly casting?
A2: A positive stop is an abrupt, 
decisive stop of the rod at the 
completion of the casting stroke — 
producing maximum rod rebound and 
line speed. Jim Green described 
this as the POSITIVE STOP. The 
greater the squeeze during the stop, 
the greater the pullback — a 
natural by-product of the stop that 
reduces counter flex and increases 
line speed. The three-point grip 
promotes a relaxed hand position 
that makes greater pullback easier 
to achieve than the classic 
thumb-on-top grip.

Q3: What is the flip the tip 
drill for roll casting?
A3: The flip the tip drill — 
credited to Carol Green and used 
by Hugh Falkus in the late 1960s 
— sends a small wave down the line 
using a tiny wrist flick combined 
with squeeze, positive stop, and 
pullback. Done slowly enough that 
the wave only travels halfway down 
the line at first, then progressively 
further. A few inches of wrist flick 
motion can send an extraordinary 
amount of line forward — a 40-foot 
roll cast becomes easy once the 
flip the tip drill is mastered.

Q4: What is the difference between 
a static and dynamic roll cast?
A4: A static roll cast lifts the 
rod into position slowly, pauses, 
then executes the forward stroke 
from a stationary line position — 
the purest test of rod loading 
mechanics. A dynamic roll cast 
sweeps a V loop or D loop behind 
the rod before the forward delivery 
— creating greater line speed and 
forming the foundation of Spey 
casting. Master the static roll 
cast first — the better your 
static roll cast becomes, the more 
easily your dynamic roll cast and 
aerial distance cast will develop.
