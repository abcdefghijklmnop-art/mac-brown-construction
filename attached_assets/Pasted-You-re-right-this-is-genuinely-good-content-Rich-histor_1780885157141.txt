Q1: What does FAIL mean in fly fishing?
A1: FAIL is an acronym for First Attempt 
In Learning — the philosophy that failure 
on the water is not a setback but a 
prerequisite for improvement. The more 
you fail, the closer success becomes. 
Every missed strike, every refusal, every 
poor cast is data — the raw material of 
a better angler. The best fly fishers 
and casting instructors are defined by 
their willingness to fail publicly, ask 
questions freely, and remain students 
of the sport regardless of their 
credentials.

Q2: Who was Jim Green and why did 
he matter to fly fishing?
A2: Jim Green — born in 1920 — was 
one of the most important figures in 
modern fly fishing. He started the 
graphite rod revolution at Fenwick, 
worked for years at the Sunset Line 
Company, and held the World Champion 
casting title with numerous casting 
merits. He was one of Mac Brown's 
biggest influences as a teenager — 
writing six-page letters explaining 
fly casting mechanics in detail, 
generously sharing 43 years of 
accumulated knowledge with a young 
student who simply asked questions. 
He was a legend not just for what 
he knew but for how freely he shared it.

Q3: What did the NCWRC creel study 
reveal about fly fisher self-assessment?
A3: The 1993 NCWRC creel study on the 
Upper Nantahala River found that over 
40% of anglers surveyed claimed to be 
advanced intermediates and 35% claimed 
to be advanced — yet the top 0.003% 
of anglers caught significantly more 
fish than the rest. Over three-quarters 
of fly fishers surveyed thought much 
more highly of their abilities than 
their actual results showed. Pride 
is the most consistent and least 
acknowledged obstacle to improvement 
in fly fishing.

Q4: What is the Daiwl Bach fly pattern 
and where does it come from?
A4: The Daiwl Bach — Welsh for "little 
devil" — is a soft hackle wet fly that 
has been fishing for nearly 400 years 
in Ireland and Wales. Mac Brown first 
encountered it as an eight-year-old 
when a skilled tobacco farmer on Camp 
Creek in Greene County, Tennessee handed 
him a handful of Red Tagged Daiwl Bachs. 
For decades Mac thought the farmer had 
invented the pattern. It remains one 
of the most consistently productive 
wet fly patterns on the delayed harvest 
sections of Western North Carolina.

Q5: How do I find a fly fishing mentor 
in North Carolina?
A5: The fastest path is quality 
instruction from a certified professional 
— someone who has made the same journey 
and can compress decades of water time 
into a single lesson. Mac Brown Fly Fish 
in Bryson City, NC offers private casting 
lessons, guided trips, and the week-long 
Fly Fishing Guide School — all built on 
the same philosophy of generosity with 
knowledge that Jim Green modeled. The 
best mentors are those who ask nothing 
in return but genuine effort from 
their students.
