Q1: What does matching the hatch mean 
in fly fishing?
A1: Matching the hatch means selecting 
an artificial fly that closely imitates 
the color, size, shape, and behavior 
of the natural insects trout are actively 
feeding on at a specific moment. When 
a hatch is occurring trout become 
selective — keying on one stage of one 
insect and refusing everything else. 
The challenge is identifying not just 
the species but the stage: nymph, 
emerger, winged adult, or spent spinner. 
Each requires a different fly and often 
a different presentation.

Q2: How do I know what insects are 
hatching on the water?
A2: Four methods — in order of 
reliability. First, observe rise forms: 
a splashy rise usually indicates caddis 
pupa; a slow sip indicates spent 
spinners or emergers in the film; a 
head-and-tail rise indicates duns. 
Second, look at the water surface for 
drifting insects. Third, check 
streamside vegetation and the air for 
flying adults. Fourth, use a stomach 
pump on a caught fish to see exactly 
what it has been eating — the most 
definitive method available.

Q3: Is matching the hatch always 
necessary for catching trout?
A3: No — and it is often overrated. 
Drift matters more than pattern in 
the vast majority of fishing situations. 
A perfectly matched fly with micro 
drag will be refused. An attractor 
pattern with a perfect drag-free 
drift will be eaten. Attractor patterns 
with hot spots, colored triggers, and 
rubber legs consistently outperform 
precise imitations on the wild streams 
of the Smoky Mountains — which is why 
Mac Brown's hatch chart recommends 
attractor elements even on imitative 
patterns.

Q4: What are the most important hatches 
to match in the Smoky Mountains?
A4: Caddisflies are the most abundant 
insect family in the region — over 330 
species identified in the surrounding 
watershed. Match caddis in larval, 
pupal, and adult forms year-round. 
Spring brings Quill Gordons, Blue 
Quills, and Hendricksons. Fall is 
defined by the October Caddis. Winter 
produces midge and BWO hatches on 
mild overcast days. Water temperature 
is the most reliable predictor of 
hatch activity — the full hatch chart 
for Western North Carolina is 
available at macbrownflyfish.com.

Q5: What is a stomach pump and 
should I use one?
A5: A stomach pump is a small rubber 
bulb with a soft tube used to extract 
stomach contents from a live trout 
without harming it — allowing the 
angler to see exactly what the fish 
has been eating. Used correctly and 
carefully it is the most definitive 
hatch-matching tool available. Mac 
Brown collected over 14,000 stomach 
samples from wild regional trout 
over 40 years to build the Bryson 
City Area Hatch Chart — the most 
comprehensive hatch data available 
for Western North Carolina.
