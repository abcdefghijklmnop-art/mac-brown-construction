FAQ — add before footer as expandable accordion:
Q1: Is fly fishing good in the Smoky 
Mountains in winter?
A1: Yes — winter is underrated on these 
waters. Blue-winged olive and midge hatches 
provide genuine dry fly opportunity on 
overcast mild days. The Tuckasegee delayed 
harvest section fishes well all winter. 
The Nantahala tailwater maintains consistent 
temperatures year-round. The biggest 
advantage is solitude — some of the finest 
trout water in the eastern United States 
with almost no other anglers on it.

Q2: What flies work best for winter trout 
fishing in the Smokies?
A2: Keep them small. CDC puff dries, 
parachute dries, and wets for emergers 
for surface activity. Pheasant tails, 
Ju-Ju's, Egan's Rainbow Warrior, and 
Iron Lotus for subsurface. Three-fly rigs 
allow you to grid off water efficiently 
and find the depth and pattern the fish 
want. Most important rule: size down 
further than you think necessary.

Q3: What tippet should I use for winter 
trout fishing?
A3: Use 6-8X fluorocarbon for subsurface 
fishing in most winter conditions — finer 
tippet sinks faster and is less visible 
in low clear water. For fishing higher 
in the film, 6-8X nylon works well. 
Carry mud to sink the tippet and Mucillen 
to float it — making fine adjustments 
based on where the fish are feeding.

Q4: Where do trout hold in winter in 
the Smoky Mountains?
A4: Cold water sends trout to slower, 
deeper lies. Fish long deep pools, slack 
water behind structure, and tailouts where 
food concentrates. Avoid fast broken water 
in cold conditions — trout cannot afford 
the energy expenditure. Water temperature 
is the guide — the warmer the water 
within the winter range, the more active 
the fish and the wider their feeding lanes.

Q5: What is the best time of day for 
winter fly fishing in the Smokies?
A5: The warmest part of the afternoon — 
typically 11am to 3pm — when water 
temperatures have climbed as high as they 
will for the day. BWO and midge hatches 
on the Tuckasegee and Nantahala most 
often occur on overcast mild days during 
this window. Early morning in winter is 
the least productive time — unlike summer 
when early morning is essential.

"Dry fly fishing for trout requires mastering 
active and passive presentations, curve and 
pile casts, drag-free drift at distance, and

and wild Smoky Mountain streams since 1987 
— and considers the dry fly the most 
rewarding and technically demanding 
discipline in fly fishing. Most anglers 
never develop it because tight lining with 
nymphs produces fish faster. That is exactly 
why the dry fly separates serious anglers 
from the rest."
