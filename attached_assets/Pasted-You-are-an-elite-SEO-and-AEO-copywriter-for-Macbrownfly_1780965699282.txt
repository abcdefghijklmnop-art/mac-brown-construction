### Why should dry fly anglers slow down their cast?

**Slowing down the cast is the number one correction Mac Brown makes with dry fly anglers.** A delivery that is too fast causes the fly to turn over too quickly — making it nearly impossible to find the dry fly on the water. A slow, smooth delivery gives you finesse, better loop control, and far more time to track the fly on the surface.

---

### What are the three main delivery casts used in dry fly fishing?

Dry fly fishing relies on three primary delivery casts — **curve cast right**, **curve cast left**, and the **pile cast**. A straight cast is used only on rare occasions when targeting a rising fish with no current interference. Building slack into the presentation through curves and pile casts is the most effective way to achieve drag-free drift before the fly even lands.

---

### How does approach angle affect dry fly success?

**Approach angle determines how much conflicting current your fly line crosses before reaching the fish.** The wrong angle means drag sets in immediately after the fly lands, regardless of how good the cast is. Look more and cast less — identify the fish's position and feeding lane first, then select the approach angle that keeps your line out of competing currents.

---

### How do you fish dry flies during winter BWO hatches?

**Winter BWO dry fly fishing is most productive on overcast, mild afternoons** when blue-winged olive hatches bring trout to the surface. After hooking a fish from a feeding pod, rest the water — do not cast back into the pod immediately. Each hookup alerts the remaining fish, and impatient re-casting puts them down fast. Patience between hookups is the single biggest advantage a winter dry fly angler can have.

---

### How do you identify quality trout from their rise forms?

**Large, high-quality trout take dry flies with very little surface disturbance** — a subtle sip with minimal rings. Small fish create a disproportionately large splash relative to their size. Learning to distinguish a quiet, confident rise from an aggressive, splashy one — a skill Mac Brown's friend Jim Estes taught him over three decades ago — lets you target the biggest fish in the pod rather than wasting presentations on small trout.
