Q1: What is the invisible pulley 
concept in fly casting?
A1: The invisible pulley is a teaching 
concept developed by Al Buhr for 
understanding where force is accented 
during the fly casting stroke — rod 
in power when the casting stroke is 
actively generating the loop, rod out 
of power when the rod is repositioning 
for the next stroke. Most new fly 
casters spend all their time throwing 
line — the invisible pulley concept 
shifts understanding from throwing 
to directing energy. It explains 
how circles, figure-eights, and oval 
casting motions use varying throttle 
positions for different casting results.

Q2: Who developed FFI continuing 
education for casting instructors?
A2: Mac Brown and David Lambert 
developed the concept of FFI continuing 
education for casting instructors around 
1995 at the Southeastern FFI Conclave 
at Callaway Gardens in Georgia. The 
format — bringing certified casting 
instructors together to share advanced 
knowledge and techniques — has since 
become standard at FFI conclaves 
globally. Mac Brown continues to 
present at FFI continuing education 
events nationally, including the 
Northwest FFI Expo and the Southeastern 
FFI Conclave.

Q3: Is Spey casting fundamentally 
different from single-hand fly casting?
A3: No — the dynamics are essentially 
identical between single and double-
handed casting. The physics of loop 
formation, rod loading, line speed, 
and tracking all apply equally. The 
challenge is that the bottom hand plays 
a large role in setting up the stroke 
correctly — a new motor pattern that 
must be ingrained before it becomes 
natural. Mac Brown describes his first 
Spey session as a great lesson in 
humility — feeling like a beginner 
all over again despite decades of 
single-hand casting mastery.

Q4: What are tube flies and why 
are they popular for steelhead 
and salmon?
A4: Tube flies are tied on hollow 
tubes rather than conventional hooks 
— allowing the hook to be replaced 
independently of the fly body, 
reducing waste when hooks dull or 
break. They also allow the hook to 
swing freely during the fight, 
improving hook-up and landing 
percentages. The tube fly revolution 
is particularly strong in the 
Pacific Northwest for winter 
steelhead and salmon fishing — 
where large Intruder-style patterns 
on tubes offer advantages in both 
material efficiency and fish-fighting 
performance.

Q5: Where can I learn fly casting 
from an FFI-certified instructor 
in the Southeast?

Instructor in North Carolina and 
a national presenter at FFI conclaves 
and The Fly Fishing Show since 1994. 
Online lessons available worldwide
